The lab gave everyone the exact same food. The only thing that changed was the time.

Researchers at Brigham and Women's Hospital had 16 people with overweight or obesity eat identical meals on two different days. On one day, meals were on an early schedule. On the other, everything was shifted about four hours later.

What they measured:

1. Hunger
On the late schedule, people felt hungrier.

2. Calories burned
They burned fewer calories after eating.

3. Fat storage
The body also showed changes consistent with storing more fat.

Same food. Different clock.

The honest limits: only 16 people took part, so this is a small study, and it doesn't prove that every late dinner does this. It does suggest timing can matter, not just calories.

(Source: Cell Metabolism, 2022, as reported by Fortune.)

If you manage blood sugar or take medication, ask your doctor before changing your meal times. For everyone else, an earlier dinner more often than not is a simple thing to try.

A practical way to read this: you don't need a perfect schedule. If most nights you eat dinner earlier than you do now, even by an hour, you've moved in the direction the study points to, without cutting a single thing from your plate.
