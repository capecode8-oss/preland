The text said $6.40. That's why she almost paid. It was small enough to feel like nothing.

My friend Lisa was in the car, waiting for her daughter's soccer practice to end. The text arrived with an official name and a link. She had a toll pass she hadn't used in a while, so it made sense. Her thumb was over the link when she stopped.

Here's what she did, and what the FBI and FTC say to do.

1. Don't tap the link
The FTC says to treat these texts as a red flag, not an invoice. The link goes to a page designed to look like your state's tolling service and collect your card details.

2. Check the real source
Look up your toll agency's website on your own, or call the number on its official page. Don't use any contact info that came in the text.

3. Report and delete
Forward the text to 7726 (SPAM) and delete it. The FBI has been tracking thousands of these complaints since 2024.

Lisa sat in the car with her heart pounding and said, "I could have given my card to a stranger in under a minute."

She told her mom the same evening. Her mom had gotten the same text the week before and nearly paid.

It isn't about being careless. Scammers build panic on purpose. The FTC's advice starts with one idea: scammers want you to react fast, so slow down. A real problem will still be there after you check the source yourself, and waiting five minutes costs nothing.
