It was one hospital and one year. The gap was still measurable.

In 2008, researchers published a study in Academic Emergency Medicine. They followed adults who arrived at an urban emergency room with sudden belly pain, between April 2004 and January 2005, and looked at who got pain medication and how fast.

What they found:

1. Women waited longer
The median wait for pain medication was 65 minutes for women and 49 minutes for men.

2. Women got opioids less often
After adjusting for age, race, triage level and pain score, women were still 13% to 25% less likely to get opioid pain relief.

3. The authors raised bias
They wrote that gender bias is a possible explanation, and suggested standard protocols for giving pain relief.

The honest limits: this was one emergency room. Not every study finds the same thing. A 2019 Australian audit found a similar gap in wait times (80 minutes for men, 94 for women), but that difference wasn't statistically significant.

(Source: Chen and colleagues, Academic Emergency Medicine, 2008.)

None of this means your doctor doesn't care. It means the way we describe pain matters. Before you go, write down three things: your pain as a number out of 10, how long it's been going on, and what it stops you from doing. Say it before they ask. It's much harder to wave away a number and a list.
