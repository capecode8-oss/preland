She said it without looking up from the chart. "It's always the same sentence."

I was waiting with a friend who'd had a scare, and the nurse on shift, 22 years in that ER, started talking while she took notes. I asked what she'd learned from all those nights.

"The women who come in too late all say the same thing: 'I didn't want to bother anyone.'"

Here's what she told me.

1. It's usually days, not hours
These women didn't just feel something tonight. They'd carried it for days, working, cooking, driving the grandkids.

2. Nobody apologizes for early
"We're there to be bothered," she said. "Nobody has ever apologized for coming in early. Many apologize for coming late."

3. What she says to do
Sudden chest pain, trouble breathing or sudden weakness: call emergency services right away. For everything else, write it down with dates and call your doctor. A list gets a real answer where "I'm tired" gets a shrug.

I realized I'd said that sentence myself. "I'll bother the doctor when it's worse." When is worse? Nobody ever told me.

When I told a friend, she said, "At our age, everything hurts." I almost believed her.

But a nurse who's seen 22 years of Monday-morning regrets said otherwise.

The biggest thing I've learned? Taking care of yourself later in life isn't "starting too late." Sometimes it's simply the first time you finally put yourself on the list.
