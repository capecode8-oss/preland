Your seat isn't the danger. Four hours of sitting completely still is.

In 2007 the World Health Organization released results of its WRIGHT study on travel and blood clots. It found the risk of a clot in a vein roughly doubles after travel of four hours or more. WHO called the absolute risk for one long trip low, about 1 in 6,000 in that report. Another summary puts it at about 1 in 4,600, so the number varies by source. The extra risk stays up for roughly four weeks after you land.

It isn't only planes. The CDC advice, as quoted by hospital sites, covers trips of more than four hours by air, car, bus or train. WHO pointed to one mechanism: blood slowing down in the leg veins when you stay immobile for hours. Risk goes higher with obesity, being very tall, oral contraceptives, inherited clotting disorders, recent surgery, hormone use, and a past clot.

What the sources I checked recommend:

1. Move
Stand and walk at least every two to three hours. An aisle seat makes that easier.

2. Stuck in your seat?
Lift your heels with your toes on the floor, then lift your toes with your heels down. Tighten and release your leg muscles. At higher risk, ask your doctor about compression stockings.

3. Know the warning signs
Leg pain, tenderness, swelling, unusual warmth or redness. Chest pain, trouble breathing or coughing up blood needs care right away.

4. Keep your seat belt on over your blanket
Crew can see it, so they don't have to wake you.

Honest note: the studies measured sitting still, not a sleeping position. I found no study showing that sleeping itself changes the risk, and for most people the risk on any one flight is small.
