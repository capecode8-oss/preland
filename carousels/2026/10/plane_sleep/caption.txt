Your seat isn't the danger. Four hours of sitting completely still is.

In 2007 the World Health Organization released results of its WRIGHT study on travel and blood clots. It found clot risk roughly doubles after travel of four hours or more. WHO called the absolute risk for one long trip low, about 1 in 6,000. The extra risk stays up for roughly four weeks after you land.

It isn't only planes. CDC advice, as quoted by hospital sites, covers trips of more than four hours by air, car, bus or train. The cause is blood slowing in the leg veins when you stay still. Risk is higher with obesity, hormone use, recent surgery, a past clot and some inherited clotting disorders.

What the sources I checked recommend:

1. Move
Stand and walk at least every two to three hours. An aisle seat makes that easier.

2. Stuck in your seat?
Lift your heels with your toes on the floor, then lift your toes with your heels down. Tighten and release your leg muscles. At higher risk, ask your doctor about compression stockings.

3. Know the warning signs
Leg pain, tenderness, swelling, unusual warmth or redness. Chest pain, trouble breathing or coughing up blood needs care right away.

4. Keep your seat belt on over your blanket
So crew don't have to wake you.

Honest note: the leg X-ray on the cover is an illustration, not a real scan. The studies measured sitting still, not a sleeping position. I found no study showing that sleeping itself changes the risk, and for most people the risk on one flight is small.
