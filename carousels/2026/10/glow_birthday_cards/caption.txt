She told me she never cried at the hearing. She cried in the card aisle.

I met Joan at a volunteer shift. She told me this story over a vending machine coffee, in the flat voice of a woman who'd told it once to a therapist and never again.

Her 14-year-old son chose his father. She didn't fight it. She thought love meant letting him go.

1. The card ritual
Every birthday for eleven years she bought him a card and wrote a message. She didn't mail it. She said mailing it felt like begging, and keeping it felt like hope.

2. The text on holidays
One line, never more. "Thinking of you." She never expected an answer and never got one.

3. The box
When he turned 25 and reached out, she gave him a shoebox with eleven cards and one more envelope: "When you're ready, I'm here. No questions."

They talked for four hours. He kept the box.

I asked what the eleven years cost her. She said, "I put everything on hold. My sleep, my knee, a doctor's appointment I'd moved eleven times." She finally went last spring.

Joan reminded me of my own mother: patient to the point of disappearing. I don't want that for either of us.

She still keeps one card from that box on her fridge. It says "Happy 15th. I'm proud of you whether or not you ever read this." She told me it's the one that got her through the hard years.

My mother never talked about her body. I decided to learn what mine was trying to say before it was too late to listen, and I put what I found in one place: four short books (The Belly Fat Lie, Aging Faster Than You Should, The Caffeine Trap, The 3AM Signal) and four tools.
