I thought she was avoiding me. She was avoiding the mirror.

Thirty years of friendship. Birthdays, two divorces, one funeral. So when Karen canceled lunch for the fifth time in a row, I stopped being hurt and started being worried. I drove over with a pot of soup.

She opened the door in her robe at 2 in the afternoon.

Here's what she told me on the porch.

1. She'd been hiding it
For eight months she'd been exhausted, achy and foggy. She told no one, not even her daughter. "Everyone else has it worse," she said.

2. She'd stopped going out
It wasn't that she didn't want to see me. She didn't want me to see that she wasn't herself.

3. She needed one person to ask twice
Not "how are you?" The first answer is always "fine." The second question, "no, really," is the one that opens the door.

I didn't try to fix anything. We wrote down everything she'd noticed, with dates. Then I drove her to the appointment.

When she'd told another friend what she was feeling, the friend said, "At our age, that's just life." Karen almost believed it.

But it wasn't just life. It was something her doctor could actually work with.

The biggest thing I've learned? Taking care of yourself later in life isn't "starting too late." Sometimes it's simply the first time you finally put yourself on the list.

I gave my family everything and kept nothing for my own body. When I finally listened to it, I wrote down what it had been telling me for years, and put it in one place: four short books (The Belly Fat Lie, Aging Faster Than You Should, The Caffeine Trap, The 3AM Signal) and four tools.
