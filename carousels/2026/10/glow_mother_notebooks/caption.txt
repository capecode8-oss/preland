My brother found the key in a coffee can. That's the part I can't forget.

My mother raised us on a nurse's schedule and a nurse's salary. In 31 years I never once heard her say "I'm not feeling well." She'd say "I'm fine, honey" and hand you the casserole.

After the funeral, we opened the drawer she'd kept locked since I was in grade school. Inside: 31 small notebooks, tied with a rubber band.

I expected recipes. Or letters. It was her body.

1. The small things
"Knee bad again." "Dizzy on the stairs." "Tired. Didn't tell Tom, he worries." Every year, the same short lines.

2. The pattern she could see
She'd noticed everything. The dates, the mornings, the foods she thought mattered. She'd been quietly keeping a file on herself for three decades.

3. The last page
Nine months before she died: "Maybe I should mention it." No date after that. I'll never know what "it" was.

I don't know if telling someone would have changed anything. Maybe not. But she did all the hard work of noticing, and kept the answer in a drawer.

I started my own notebook that week. Mine gets read out loud, to my doctor and to my daughter.

The biggest thing I've learned? Taking care of yourself later in life isn't starting too late. Sometimes it's the first time you finally put yourself on the list.

My mother never talked about her body. I decided to learn what mine was trying to say before it was too late to listen, and I put what I found in one place: four short books (The Belly Fat Lie, Aging Faster Than You Should, The Caffeine Trap, The 3AM Signal) and four tools. That's the Health Bundle. One of the tools is a 14-day body log, the notebook I wish she'd shown someone.
