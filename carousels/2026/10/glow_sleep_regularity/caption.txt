The question isn't how long you sleep. It's whether your body ever knows when to expect it.

A study in the journal Sleep used wrist trackers on 60,977 people in the UK Biobank, measuring over 10 million hours of data. Then it followed them for up to 7.8 years.

What they found:

1. Regularity mattered
Compared with the least regular sleepers, the other four groups had a 20% to 48% lower risk of dying from any cause.

2. It beat duration
Sleep regularity was a stronger predictor of mortality than how many hours people slept.

3. Beyond death
Risks were also lower for cancer and heart-related causes.

The honest limits: people wore the tracker for just one week, so it's a snapshot, and it's observational. It shows a link, not proof that changing your bedtime adds years.

(Source: Windred and colleagues, Sleep, 2023.)

A simple thing to try: pick a wake-up time you can keep, including weekends, and let bedtime follow. No special equipment.

Notice what this study didn't say. It didn't say sleep less, and it didn't say hours don't matter. It said the pattern matters too. A body that knows when night starts may simply cope better than a body that guesses.

Think about your own week for a second. If your weekday alarm and your Saturday wake-up are three hours apart, your body is getting a tiny dose of jet lag every weekend, with no flight to blame.
