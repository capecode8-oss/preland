She told me the worst part wasn't the money. It was how real the crying sounded.

My neighbor Dolores is 71. At 6 AM her phone rang. A young voice, sobbing: "Grandma, I'm in trouble. Please don't tell Mom." She was already in her coat with $4,000 from the cookie tin when she remembered the rule.

Here's what saved her, and what she told me to share.

1. A family code word
Months before, her granddaughter had made her agree on one. If it's really me, I'll say it. The caller never did. When Dolores asked, the line went dead.

2. Slow it down
Scammers want you in a rush, in secret and scared. Every part of that is a tell. Ask for the code word, then hang up.

3. Call back on the number you already have
Not the one that just called. Call the person directly, or someone who knows where they are.

She sat down on the bottom stair for a long time. Then she called her granddaughter, who was asleep in her dorm.

When Dolores told her friends the story, one said, "At your age, it's easy to get fooled." Dolores said, "At my age I finally know what to check."

She told me the scariest part wasn't the voice. It was how natural it felt to obey it. Panic switches off the part of you that asks questions, and the scammer knows that.

The biggest thing she said she learned? Taking care of yourself isn't "starting too late." Sometimes it's the first time you finally put yourself on the list.
