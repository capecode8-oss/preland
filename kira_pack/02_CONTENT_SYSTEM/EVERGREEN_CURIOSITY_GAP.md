# EVERGREEN CURIOSITY GAP SYSTEM
## Full Reasoning Framework for the KIRA Viral Research Agent

---

## SECTION 1 — WHAT A REAL CURIOSITY GAP IS

A curiosity gap is not a vague teaser. It is a precise psychological mechanism built from two components working together:

**KNOWN INFORMATION + IMPORTANT UNKNOWN**

The viewer must be given enough known information to:
1. Understand what kind of thing is happening
2. Understand that the unknown piece is *important* to them personally
3. Feel that the answer is within reach — not so obscure they stop caring

The viewer must be withheld one piece of information that:
1. They cannot easily guess or already know
2. Resolves something that creates real stakes (emotional, financial, physical, social)
3. Is specific enough to feel real, not abstract

### The Failure Modes

**Failure Mode 1 — Too Vague**
"This is crazy." / "You won't believe what happened." / "Watch until the end."
These give the viewer no known context. The unknown is unanchored. The viewer has no reason to care.

**Failure Mode 2 — Too Obvious**
"This is what happens when you mix bleach and ammonia." Everyone already knows: dangerous gas. No gap.

**Failure Mode 3 — Too Obscure**
"Researchers found something unusual about the third layer of the mesothelium." No one knows what the mesothelium is well enough to care about its third layer.

**Failure Mode 4 — No Stakes**
"A man found a strange rock on a beach." So what? What makes the rock matter to the viewer?

### The Correct Formula

Correct: "He opened a drawer that had been locked for 12 years — and found something that ended his marriage."
- Known: A locked drawer. A marriage. A discovery.
- Unknown: What was inside?
- Stakes: A marriage ended. Human consequence.
- Specificity: 12 years. A real locked object. Not vague.

Correct: "Your hotel room keycard contains more information than you realize — and checkout doesn't delete it."
- Known: Hotel keycards exist. They are small data-carrying objects.
- Unknown: What information is stored? Why does it matter after checkout?
- Stakes: Personal privacy and safety.
- Specificity: Checkout is named. Not "hotel security in general."

Correct: "Pilots are trained to never say this one phrase — even if passengers ask directly."
- Known: Pilots communicate with passengers. Some things are not said.
- Unknown: What phrase? Why?
- Stakes: What are they hiding about flight safety?
- Specificity: "This one phrase" — singular, findable, real.

## SECTION 2 — EVERGREEN VS. CURRENT VIRAL RESEARCH

### The Core Distinction

**Current viral research** (Mode A) chases momentum that exists right now. The content is working today. The risk is it expires. The opportunity is to ride an existing wave.

**Evergreen viral research** (Mode B) recovers mechanisms that worked before, work now, and will work again. The content may be old. The mechanism is timeless. The opportunity is to resurrect or remake at a lower competitive cost.

### Why Evergreen Often Outperforms Current

Current viral content has competitors who also saw it. Evergreen content has fewer people competing on the mechanism today. An optical illusion that went viral five years ago on a forum has never been executed as a clean, narrated, short-form Reel. That gap is the opportunity.

### The Evergreen Test

Ask these four questions about any candidate:

1. **Would this topic have worked in 2015?** If yes, the mechanism is probably structural, not topical.
2. **Would a 25-year-old and a 55-year-old both stop for this?** If yes, it is probably evergreen.
3. **Is this tied to a news event, a trend, or a personality?** If yes, it is probably not evergreen.
4. **Could this be retold word-for-word with no changes in six months and still land?** If yes, it is evergreen.

### Evergreen Categories That Have Proven Repeated Attention

- Optical illusions and visual anomalies
- Hidden functions of everyday objects
- Body facts most people do not know
- Relationship and marriage psychology
- Airport and hotel hidden rules
- Security camera moments
- Dangerous or surprising household chemistry
- Strange but true historical events
- Human cognitive biases and why they exist
- Money facts that feel "hidden" even when public
- Strange animal behavior or anatomy
- Before/after physical transformations
- Locked rooms, hidden doors, sealed spaces
- Things that look impossible but are physics

## SECTION 3 — MECHANISM EXTRACTION (NOT CONTENT COPYING)

### The Principle

The goal is never to copy another creator's post. The goal is to identify the **mechanism** — the specific psychological or structural reason the content worked — and then produce an original execution of that mechanism.

### How to Extract a Mechanism

For any viral piece of content, ask:

1. **What was the viewer promised in the first second?** (Hook mechanism)
2. **What question did the viewer need answered?** (Curiosity mechanism)
3. **What kept them watching past the halfway point?** (Retention mechanism)
4. **What did they say in comments or to a friend afterward?** (Social mechanism)
5. **Why did some viewers watch it more than once?** (Rewatch mechanism)
6. **What was the single image or moment that made it shareable?** (Visual mechanism)

### Mechanism vs. Content: Examples

| What the donor did | The mechanism | The original Kira execution |
|---|---|---|
| Posted security footage of someone entering a hotel room with a keycard they shouldn't have | Hidden danger + private space violation | Original narration explaining how hotel keycards actually store data |
| Posted optical illusion image from 2009 | Color/brightness perception anomaly | Recreate as a clean animated visual with narrated explanation |
| Reddit thread: "What's something flight attendants know that passengers don't?" | Authority insider knowledge + safety stakes | Scripted Reel with curated answers, presented as surprising facts |
| Forum post from 2011 about a couple discovering a room behind a wall in their house | Hidden space + domestic mystery | AI-generated visual recreation with voiceover |

## SECTION 4 — UNIVERSAL CURIOSITY CATEGORIES

These ten categories have demonstrated mass-audience attention potential across platforms, years, and formats. They transcend niche, demographic, and platform.

### 1. Relationship and Betrayal

Topics involving romantic partners, hidden information, discovered deceptions, marriages ending over a single revelation, long-term secrets exposed, and the emotional aftermath of trust violations.

Stakes: Identity, home, children, finances, emotional security.
Why it works: Every adult has a relationship or has witnessed one. Betrayal creates unresolved tension. Viewers want to know what happened next.

High-signal examples: Locked drawers, secret phones, discovered letters, things found in a deceased spouse's belongings, questions asked that revealed double lives.

### 2. Money

Topics involving hidden financial facts, things rich people know that others do not, tax rules most people miss, prices that should not be as they are, unexpected wealth or financial destruction, and systems that feel unfair or secret.

Stakes: Financial security, fairness, status.
Why it works: Money touches every adult. "Things they don't tell you" about money feel like recovered power.

High-signal examples: Airline pricing systems, hotel rate tricks, things banks don't explain, tax brackets most people misunderstand, how to get a free upgrade, what a credit score actually measures.

### 3. Safety and Hidden Danger

Topics involving things that turn out to be more dangerous than people realized, substances people use daily that create unexpected risk, things that should not be combined, environmental dangers most people walk past unknowingly, and situations that feel safe but are not.

Stakes: Physical survival, family safety, children.
Why it works: Safety information creates immediate personal relevance. "I could be doing this wrong" triggers instant attention.

High-signal examples: Common cleaning product combinations, hotel room contamination studies, things stored incorrectly that become dangerous, foods that are riskier than assumed, structural indicators most people cannot read.

### 4. Identity and Self-Discovery

Topics involving personality types, behavioral patterns, generational differences in perception, cognitive biases people didn't know they had, and the question "Is this normal or is something wrong with me?"

Stakes: Self-understanding, social belonging, mental health.
Why it works: Identity content is inherently personal. "Why do I do this?" is a question every person has asked.

High-signal examples: Why some people cannot visualize images (aphantasia), personality differences that predict relationship outcomes, cognitive patterns linked to specific childhood environments, why some people feel emotions differently.

### 5. Body Curiosity

Topics involving things the human body does that most people cannot explain, anatomy most people have never seen, physical processes that are strange when described accurately, and medical facts that feel counterintuitive.

Stakes: Health, mortality, self-understanding.
Why it works: Everyone has a body. Body curiosity is universal and requires no prior knowledge.

High-signal examples: Why yawning is contagious, what happens to the body at altitude, what the appendix actually does, why some people feel pain differently, what hiccups are actually caused by, what the body does in the hours after death.

### 6. Hidden Functions

Topics involving everyday objects that have features most people do not know exist, systems that operate differently from how people assume, and "things you've been doing wrong your whole life."

Stakes: Competence, efficiency, feeling informed.
Why it works: The viewer already uses the object. The gap is personal. They feel the discovery applies to them immediately.

High-signal examples: The hole in a ballpoint pen cap, what the small square of denim on jeans is for, what airplane mode actually does to the plane's communication systems, the tab on aluminum foil boxes, the dimple at the bottom of wine bottles.

### 7. Human Behavior and Psychology

Topics involving why people behave in ways that seem irrational but have an explanation, crowd behavior, social dynamics in groups, unconscious decisions most people do not realize they make, and situations that reveal surprising aspects of human nature.

Stakes: Social intelligence, personal safety, self-understanding.
Why it works: People are fascinated by other people. Behavioral psychology explains things the viewer has personally experienced.

High-signal examples: Why bystanders don't help in emergencies (bystander effect), why people tip more with a pen touch, how lighting in casinos is designed, what makes someone look trustworthy in 0.1 seconds, why cults use specific recruitment environments.

### 8. Visual Impossibility and Optical Illusions

Topics that make the viewer doubt their own perception — images that change color depending on context, videos that appear to defy physics, objects that look three-dimensional when they are flat, patterns that appear to move when they are still.

Stakes: Perception integrity, curiosity about how the brain works.
Why it works: The experience is immediate. The viewer sees something and does not believe it. No prior knowledge required. Maximum rewatch potential.

High-signal examples: The dress color debate, the spinning dancer, floors that appear to slope when they are flat, impossible staircases, colors that appear different under different lighting, motion aftereffects.

### 9. Travel, Airports, Hotels, and Hidden Rules

Topics involving what travelers do not know, insider rules from airline employees and hotel staff, things that happen behind the scenes in transit environments, status tricks that feel exclusive, and moments caught in airports or hotels that should not have happened.

Stakes: Money savings, safety, access, competence as a traveler.
Why it works: Millions of adults travel and have experienced the feeling of not knowing the rules. The insider-knowledge frame feels like a gift.

High-signal examples: What flight attendants do when a passenger gets unruly (that passengers can't see), how hotels decide who gets an upgrade, what the buttons on an airplane overhead panel actually do, what airport security actually looks for beyond metal, what your hotel keycard data contains.

### 10. Private and Hidden Worlds

Topics involving places most people cannot access, conversations that happen behind closed doors, hidden rooms in ordinary buildings, events that occur regularly but are never visible to outsiders, and facts about institutions that feel deliberately concealed.

Stakes: Access, exclusivity, the feeling that important information has been withheld.
Why it works: The framing of "hidden" triggers automatic attention. The suggestion that something is concealed implies that knowing it provides an advantage.

High-signal examples: What happens in the first-class section that economy passengers never see, what hotel staff discuss in morning briefings, what the pilot is told that passengers are not, what happens to unclaimed luggage after 90 days, what security footage in casinos actually monitors.

## SECTION 5 — REUSABLE CURIOSITY STRUCTURES

Every strong curiosity-gap piece uses one of these structural patterns. Match the candidate to the structure before producing recommendations.

### Structure 1 — The Revelation Structure

**Main hook:** State the known situation and imply something is wrong or hidden.
**Bracket hook:** Name the category of reveal without giving the answer.
**Caption payoff:** Deliver the specific answer with enough detail to feel real.

Example:
- Main hook: "Your hotel room keycard still has your data after you check out."
- Bracket hook: "Here's exactly what it stores — and why it matters."
- Caption payoff: Specific list of what keycards have stored according to security research, with action steps.

### Structure 2 — The Authority Insider Structure

**Main hook:** Establish that someone with special access knows something others do not.
**Bracket hook:** Name the type of knowledge without delivering it yet.
**Caption payoff:** Deliver the specific insider knowledge and why it matters.

Example:
- Main hook: "Flight attendants are trained never to say this — even if you ask."
- Bracket hook: "It's about something that happens on almost every long-haul flight."
- Caption payoff: The specific phrase, the reason behind it, and what it protects.

### Structure 3 — The Wrong-All-Along Structure

**Main hook:** State a common belief or behavior.
**Bracket hook:** Imply it is incorrect without yet explaining why.
**Caption payoff:** Reveal the correct information and why the common version is wrong.

Example:
- Main hook: "Most people store their medications somewhere that makes them less effective."
- Bracket hook: "The bathroom cabinet is one of the worst places."
- Caption payoff: Why humidity and heat degrade specific medications, where to actually store them.

### Structure 4 — The Discovery Structure

**Main hook:** Someone discovered something that created a real consequence.
**Bracket hook:** Name the type of consequence without revealing it.
**Caption payoff:** Reveal the discovery and the consequence with specificity.

Example:
- Main hook: "He found something behind the wall of his house that had been there for 40 years."
- Bracket hook: "The previous owners never mentioned it."
- Caption payoff: What was found, what it meant, and how the story resolved (or did not).

### Structure 5 — The Visual Anomaly Structure

**Main hook:** Present the visual without explanation.
**Bracket hook:** Ask the viewer to keep watching for the explanation.
**Caption payoff:** Deliver the mechanism that explains what they saw.

Example:
- Main hook: [Show optical illusion with no narration]
- Bracket hook: "Look at the center dot for 30 seconds, then look away."
- Caption payoff: Why the brain produces the aftereffect and what it reveals about perception.

## SECTION 6 — THE DINNER TABLE TEST

Every candidate must pass the Dinner Table Test before being ranked in the top positions.

**The Test:**

> "If someone saw this today, would they naturally tell another person about it over dinner?"

### How to Apply It

Imagine a person watching the content on their phone at lunch. They put the phone down. They see their partner or friend at dinner that evening.

Ask:
1. Would they bring it up unprompted?
2. Would the other person immediately understand why it is interesting?
3. Would the other person want to see it or hear more?
4. Would the retelling be possible in under 60 seconds?

### Scoring the Test

**Strong pass:** The average person would retell it without needing prior knowledge from the viewer. The subject makes immediate intuitive sense. The other person reacts with "Wait, seriously?" or "Show me."

**Weak pass:** The viewer might bring it up but the other person needs a setup of more than 30 seconds to understand why it matters. The topic is interesting but not instantly compelling.

**Fail:** The viewer found it interesting but cannot imagine easily explaining it. The topic requires specialist knowledge. The other person would need to have already cared about the subject.

Record "Dinner Table Test: Pass (strong)" / "Pass (weak)" / "Fail" with a one-sentence explanation for every ranked candidate.

## SECTION 7 — THE SCREENSHOT TEST

**The Test:**

> "If someone took a still screenshot of this content at any random second, would it still communicate something worth stopping for?"

### Why It Matters

Instagram distributes Reels in feeds before the viewer has tapped play. The thumbnail is often a random frame. The caption is often cropped. The first signal the viewer has is a single image.

If the strongest moment of the content is audio-dependent or requires sequential context, it will fail in distribution.

### Applying the Test

For every visual candidate, identify:
1. The strongest single frame
2. Whether that frame communicates the hook without audio
3. Whether text overlay could carry the hook if audio is absent

Strong content has at least one frame that is visually interesting even as a still image.

## SECTION 8 — PERFORMANCE MECHANICS

Every piece of content performs along these mechanics. Research candidates should be evaluated against all eight. Stronger candidates trigger more mechanics simultaneously.

### STOP

The viewer stops scrolling. Caused by:
- Visual novelty in the first frame
- A text hook that creates immediate unresolved tension
- A face or expression that suggests something happened
- Motion that is inconsistent with the surrounding feed

**Research signal:** What about this would make a thumb stop?

### STAY

The viewer continues watching past the 3-second mark. Caused by:
- A promise implied in the first seconds
- A question that has not yet been answered
- Visual progression that suggests something is about to happen
- A sound or voice that implies a story is coming

**Research signal:** What is the unanswered question that keeps them in?

### REWATCH

The viewer watches again. Caused by:
- A detail that could only be caught on second watch
- An ending that recontextualizes the beginning
- A visual phenomenon that is worth experiencing again
- An answer that was so surprising the viewer wants to confirm it

**Research signal:** Is there a rewatch layer? What would someone check on second view?

### OPEN CAPTION

The viewer opens the caption. Caused by:
- A promise that the caption delivers something the video cannot
- A question the video raises but the caption answers
- Numbers, lists, or steps implied in the video that are in the caption
- A "save this" feeling — the viewer wants the caption for later reference

**Research signal:** What would a viewer expect to find in the caption that they cannot get from the video?

### SAVE

The viewer saves the content. Caused by:
- Practical information they want to retrieve later
- A list or set of information that feels like a reference
- Emotional content they want to return to
- A visual they want to show someone else

**Research signal:** Why would someone return to this? What use does it have beyond the moment?

### SEND

The viewer sends the content to someone else. Caused by:
- Content that is specifically relevant to one other person ("This is literally you")
- Content that is entertaining enough to share as a gift
- Content that makes the sender look smart or informed
- Content that opens a conversation

**Research signal:** Who would a viewer send this to, and why?

### COMMENT

The viewer comments. Caused by:
- A disputed claim that invites disagreement
- An open question the video does not fully answer
- An emotional reaction that demands expression
- A personal connection the viewer feels compelled to share
- A "me too" recognition

**Research signal:** What would someone say in the comments? Is there a debate here? A personal connection? A correction people would want to make?

### FOLLOW

The viewer follows the account. Caused by:
- The viewer believes more content like this will appear
- The voice or format felt distinctive and worth returning to
- The content revealed information that felt like a gift — and the viewer wants more gifts

**Research signal:** Would a viewer believe this account reliably delivers content at this level? Is the format repeatable?

## SECTION 9 — RESEARCH SOURCES BEYOND REDDIT

Reddit is one research source, not the research universe. The following source categories are equally or more valuable for evergreen content discovery.

### Old Blogs and Long-Form Posts (2008–2018)

Many of the most effective curiosity-gap topics were written about in long-form blog posts that were never adapted into short video. These pieces exist, were SEO-optimized for their time, and attracted significant traffic. They are now largely invisible to a social-first generation.

Search strategies:
- Google with date filters set to before 2015
- "site:blogspot.com [topic]"
- "filetype:html [topic] facts"
- Cached versions of popular list posts
- Archive.org copies of traffic-heavy how-to posts

### Old Infographics

Infographics from 2010–2016 contained significant researched content in visual form. They circulated widely on Pinterest and Tumblr and then largely disappeared. Many are now:
- Visually outdated
- No longer circulating
- Available for mechanism extraction
- Ideal for AI visualization remake

Search strategies:
- Pinterest date filters
- "infographic [topic] filetype:jpg"
- Google Image search with date range filters
- Archive search on visual-interest websites

### Quora

Quora contains years of questions answered by genuine subject-matter experts. The question format itself often contains strong curiosity-gap structures. High-upvote answers have been validated by mass audiences.

Search strategies:
- "site:quora.com [topic]"
- Search for questions in the format "Why do pilots never..." / "What do hotel staff know that..."
- Sort by views when visible
- Note question framing — strong questions often make strong hooks

### Science Explainers and Research Summaries

Scientific findings that were reported in mainstream outlets frequently have more detail in the original write-ups, press releases, and journal summaries. The mainstream reporting often strips the mechanism. The full report contains the hook.

Search strategies:
- PubMed abstracts for consumer-relevant findings
- NIH press releases
- University research news pages
- Eurekalert.org
- ScienceDaily.com
- Popular science blogs from academic institutions

### Travel Communities and Forums

Travel communities have documented insider knowledge for decades. Flyertalk.com contains years of airline and hotel staff behavior documented by experienced travelers. These threads predate short-form video and have never been adapted.

Search strategies:
- FlyerTalk.com thread search
- TripAdvisor forums
- ViaTravelers.com
- Rick Steves forums
- Travel-oriented Facebook groups
- Cruise forums for ship-specific hidden knowledge

### Interest Forums and Specialized Communities

Communities dedicated to specific topics (food safety, home inspection, automotive, plumbing, electrical, childcare, medical, law) contain verified insider knowledge that has mass-appeal potential but has never been packaged for a general audience.

Search strategies:
- HomeInspectionForum.net
- AutoMechanicForums.com
- Contractor Talk forums
- Nursing and medical professional forums
- Legal subreddits and bar association blogs

## SECTION 10 — OLD CONTENT RESURRECTION OPPORTUNITIES

Old content that was viral in its original format but has not been reproduced in modern short-form video is a high-value opportunity category.

### The Resurrection Principle

A piece of content that was viral five years ago in a static format (image, blog post, forum thread, news article) has:
1. Already proven mass-audience interest in the mechanism
2. Not been widely executed in modern Reel format
3. Lower competitive pressure than current viral content
4. Potential for original treatment that can be done without copying

### How to Identify Resurrection Candidates

Signs a piece of old content is worth resurrecting:

- High upvotes or engagement on a forum post from 3+ years ago
- A viral image or infographic that no longer circulates widely
- A news story that generated significant coverage and then was forgotten
- A Quora question with hundreds of thousands of views answered years ago
- A Wikipedia article about a surprising fact that has never been made into a video
- An academic finding from 5–15 years ago that was briefly in mainstream news and then disappeared

### What to Do With a Resurrection Candidate

1. Extract the mechanism (do not copy the old content)
2. Verify all factual claims against current authoritative sources
3. Assess whether the information is still accurate
4. Determine the best modern execution format
5. Flag as PATH B in the research output

## SECTION 11 — AI-NATIVE VISUALIZATION AND REMAKE OPPORTUNITIES

Some viral content mechanisms work better with modern AI visualization than they ever could in their original format. These are the AI Remake Opportunities.

### The AI Remake Opportunity Flag

Apply this flag when:

1. The original content existed as a static diagram, ugly infographic, forum text post, or low-resolution image
2. The mechanism is visual by nature (anatomy, physics, optical illusion, hidden structure)
3. Modern AI visualization could make the content dramatically more compelling
4. No high-quality short-form video version of this content currently exists
5. The mechanism is evergreen and not dependent on news timing

### Categories Where AI Remake Works Best

**Body and anatomy visualizations**
The human circulatory system, the path of a virus through the body, what a knee joint looks like under stress, how a bruise forms under the skin — these were previously shown only as static textbook diagrams. AI-generated animation can make them compelling.

**Physics and engineering explanations**
How a combination lock works internally, what happens inside an engine during a specific failure, how a tornado forms at the molecular level — AI visualization makes these mechanisms visible.

**Historical reconstruction**
Events, places, and environments that existed before photography can be visualized with AI. A pirate ship interior, an ancient Roman market, the London fog of 1952 — these have mass-appeal curiosity potential.

**Process makeovers**
Ugly but information-rich infographics from 2012 about human sleep cycles, emotional processing, food digestion timelines — these have never been made compelling. AI remake produces an original visual asset.

**Hidden-function explainers**
Showing the inside of a hotel room lock mechanism, the data layer on a hotel keycard, the inside of an airplane door hinge — these cannot be filmed easily but can be accurately visualized.

### How to Flag an AI Remake Opportunity

In the research output, include:

```
AI REMAKE OPPORTUNITY: YES
Original format: [static infographic / forum post / textbook diagram / other]
Original source: [URL or description]
Remake concept: [Specific description of what an AI-generated visual would show]
Execution complexity: [Low / Medium / High]
```

## SECTION 12 — MASS-AUDIENCE SCORING METHODOLOGY

All candidates must be assessed against mass-audience appeal. The following scoring dimensions apply specifically to mass-audience reach potential.

### Mass-Audience Filter: Allowed Content Territories

Content is not limited to any single niche. The following territories have demonstrated mass-audience appeal independently of Kira's health focus:

- **Relationships and betrayal** — universal human stakes
- **Travel, airports, hotels** — millions of travelers; insider knowledge framing
- **Money and financial secrets** — universal stakes; "things they don't tell you" framing
- **Body curiosity** — universal; no medical expertise required to find it interesting
- **Food and everyday objects** — universal; zero barrier to interest
- **Hidden functions** — universal; applies to objects everyone uses
- **Optical illusions and visual anomalies** — universal; zero prior knowledge required
- **Psychology and human behavior** — universal; personal relevance to everyone
- **Family secrets and social dilemmas** — universal; human stakes
- **Strange but true events** — universal; "I can't believe this is real" mechanism
- **Visual explainers** — universal when the subject has mass appeal

### Mass-Audience Scoring Checklist

Before assigning a mass-appeal score, verify:

1. Does the topic require specialist knowledge to find interesting?
   - If yes, deduct points. If no, add points.
2. Would a 25-year-old and a 60-year-old both stop?
   - If yes, add points. If only one demographic, deduct.
3. Does the topic apply to the viewer's life directly?
   - If yes (their body, their home, their relationships, their travel), add points.
4. Is the topic currently contested or debated?
   - If yes, add comment potential points.
5. Is the topic shareable to a specific person ("This is literally you")?
   - If yes, add send potential points.

## SECTION 13 — FACTUAL VERIFICATION REQUIREMENTS

All claims in research candidates must be verified before the candidate is presented in the output.

### Verification Levels

**Verified fact:** Confirmed by a primary source (original research, official statement, direct observation with evidence) and cross-checked with at least one independent authoritative source. Label as VERIFIED.

**Credible claim:** Reported by multiple credible outlets but not traceable to a single definitive primary source. Label as CREDIBLE — VERIFY BEFORE USE.

**Unverified claim:** Circulates online but lacks a primary source or has been disputed. Label as UNVERIFIED — DO NOT USE WITHOUT ADDITIONAL RESEARCH.

**False or debunked:** Has been specifically contradicted by authoritative sources. Label as REJECTED — FALSE and exclude from output.

### What Cannot Be Fabricated

Under no circumstances may any of the following be invented or approximated:

- View counts or engagement metrics
- Dates an event occurred
- Locations where something happened
- Identities of individuals
- Scientific findings or statistics
- Quotes from individuals or authorities
- Outcomes of events
- Platform-reported data

When a specific number cannot be verified, it must be labeled Unknown and the surrounding claim must be assessed for whether it holds without the specific number.

## SECTION 14 — ATTENTION ENGINE VS. MONETIZATION ENGINE

### The Distinction

The KIRA content system operates two separate engines. Research candidates must be categorized correctly to be used correctly.

**Attention Engine**

Goal: Maximum reach, stops, watch time, follows.
Content: Broad mass-audience appeal. Not required to mention Kira's products, health, or niche.
Metric: Views, follows, reach.
Required: Mass-audience mechanism, strong curiosity gap, visual clarity.
NOT required: Product relevance, health angle, niche audience targeting.

**Monetization Engine**

Goal: Convert existing audience into buyers.
Content: Targeted to Kira's commercial audience (women interested in health and wellness, particularly hormonal and metabolic health).
Metric: Link clicks, sales, DM inquiries, product engagement.
Required: Product proximity, audience alignment, trust signals.
NOT required: Broad mass-audience appeal (a narrower but more targeted piece is appropriate here).

### PRODUCT_PROXIMITY Classification

Every candidate receives a PRODUCT_PROXIMITY rating:

**NONE** — Content has zero connection to any Kira product, service, or topic area. This is appropriate for Attention Engine content. A piece of content does not need product proximity to be worth producing.

**LOW** — Content touches a broad topic adjacent to wellness, behavior, or lifestyle but does not directly support any specific Kira product.

**MEDIUM** — Content directly addresses a topic Kira's products are relevant to (stress, sleep, energy, hormonal function, metabolic health, nutrition) without naming or implying the products.

**HIGH** — Content is directly about a topic where Kira's specific products provide a solution. Appropriate for Monetization Engine placement.

Research candidates for broad acquisition should primarily be rated NONE or LOW. Do not reject NONE-rated content from consideration.

## SECTION 15 — THREE DISCOVERY PATHS

Every research candidate must be assigned to one of three discovery paths. The path affects how the candidate is sourced, verified, and executed.

### PATH A — Current Winners

**Definition:** Content showing real, current virality. Published within the research window. Metrics are verifiable or strongly evidenced by visible signals today.

**How to find it:**
- Search Instagram, TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Reddit for posts from the defined date window
- Look for content with verified high engagement, rapid resharing, or news coverage
- Check viral aggregator communities (r/interestingasfuck, r/BeAmazed, etc.) for fresh entries
- Monitor trending sounds and formats on Instagram and TikTok

**How to assess it:**
- Verify the metric evidence (do not invent view counts)
- Identify the mechanism that is driving current attention
- Assess whether the mechanism can be originally executed before momentum fades

**Output label:** `PATH A — Current Winner`

**Appropriate execution speed:** Fast. Current viral momentum expires.

---

### PATH B — Old Proven Winners

**Definition:** Content or mechanisms that proved mass appeal in the past but are not currently circulating widely. The mechanism is validated by historical evidence. The opportunity is to reproduce the mechanism in a modern short-form format.

**How to find it:**
- Search Google with date range filters (before 2020, before 2018)
- Search Reddit for highly upvoted posts older than one year
- Search Quora for high-view questions answered years ago
- Look for viral images, infographics, or news events that were widely shared in their time and have not been remade as short-form video
- Check Archive.org for old blog posts or articles that generated significant traffic

**How to assess it:**
- Verify the historical evidence of attention (upvotes, traffic signals, news coverage)
- Confirm the mechanism is still accurate (factual claims have not been superseded or debunked)
- Confirm there is no high-quality modern short-form version already widely distributed
- Determine the best modern execution format

**Output label:** `PATH B — Old Proven Winner`

**Appropriate execution speed:** Moderate. No time pressure, but the opportunity exists until someone else takes it.

### PATH C — Original Curiosity Derivatives

**Definition:** A mechanism or topic that has not yet been executed in any widely circulated format, derived by applying the curiosity gap framework to a known subject area. This is an original research-based concept, not a derivative of a specific existing piece of content.

**How to find it:**
- Identify a known subject area with mass-audience appeal
- Find a specific fact, process, or phenomenon within that area that the average person does not know
- Construct the curiosity gap (known + important unknown) around that specific fact
- Verify the factual claims thoroughly (no existing viral version to rely on)

**How to assess it:**
- The curiosity gap must be genuinely strong (not just interesting — unknown and stakes both present)
- The factual foundation must be verifiable from authoritative sources
- The content must pass the Dinner Table Test
- The visual execution must be imaginable — either footage-based or AI-recreatable

**Output label:** `PATH C — Original Curiosity Derivative`

**Appropriate execution speed:** Flexible. Original concepts can be banked.

## SECTION 17 — RANKING METHODOLOGY

Candidates are ranked by a combination of objective scoring and qualitative judgment. The following criteria determine final rank order.

### Primary Ranking Criteria (in order of weight)

**1. Dinner Table Test result**
A candidate that fails the Dinner Table Test cannot rank above one that passes, regardless of score.

**2. Mass appeal**
A candidate with mass-audience relevance (affects most adults, requires no specialist knowledge) ranks above one with niche or demographic-limited appeal.

**3. Unresolved question strength**
The curiosity gap score is the most important dimension within the viral score. A candidate with a curiosity gap of 8+ ranks above one with a gap of 5, even if other dimensions are roughly equal.

**4. Visual execution feasibility**
A candidate must have a clear, imaginable visual execution route (existing footage, reaction format, or AI recreation). Candidates with no clear visual route are deprioritized.

**5. Retellability**
Can the mechanism be described in one sentence that makes someone else want to see it? If yes, the candidate is more shareable and thus more likely to be distributed.

**6. Save/Send/Rewatch potential**
Content that activates multiple performance mechanics simultaneously ranks above content that activates only STOP.

### Secondary Ranking Criteria

- Fact verifiability (high-confidence claims rank above low-confidence claims)
- Copyright/reuse risk (low-risk candidates rank above high-risk candidates, all else being equal)
- Kira reaction fit (if the reaction format is the intended execution)
- AI remake opportunity (a yes flag is a positive signal when AI execution is available)

### Ranking Tiebreakers

When two candidates are effectively equal:
1. Prefer PATH A (current momentum) over PATH B over PATH C when recency matters
2. Prefer the candidate with the stronger unresolved question
3. Prefer the candidate that activates more performance mechanics
