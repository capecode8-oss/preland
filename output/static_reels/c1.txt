They scanned the brains of 17 people who had been married about 21 years. All 17 said they were still deeply in love.

Their reward centers lit up. The same region that fires in the first weeks of new love.

Not every long marriage looks like that. But the spark doesn't have to die.

So what do those couples do differently?

First, the "18 months" number. It's a popular estimate, not a law. Researchers like Helen Fisher and Dorothy Tennov place the obsessive, can't-eat-can't-sleep phase somewhere between a year and a few years. It fades for almost everyone. That part is normal.

What the research points to:

1. Do new things together
Psychologist Arthur Aron tested this in 2000. Couples spent 1.5 hours a week for 10 weeks on either pleasant routine or exciting, unfamiliar activities. The exciting group reported bigger gains in satisfaction. His theory: boredom drains the spark.

2. It's not the thrill. It's the growing.
A later study suggested the feeling of "we're learning something together" mattered more than raw adrenaline. A new class beats a scary ride.

3. Stop letting the scenery become wallpaper
Same restaurant. Same couch. Same Tuesday. Your brain stops registering it. Change one small thing a week.

Honest note: these are small studies. They show a pattern, not a guarantee. No scan can promise you anything.
